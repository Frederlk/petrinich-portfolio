## Patterns and decisions

- The format is the architecture. There is no software behind it: the interview is a written package of a candidate brief, five teammate briefs, a ticket set with an interviewer key, a rubric and an interviewer guide. Every trap lives in content, so changing one means editing a sentence, not code.
- A session is one 90-minute round on the candidate's own machine, with a real repository, Claude Code and a team chat. A throwaway chat server per candidate keeps the setup small, and deleting it is the cleanup.
- Each of the five teammates is one system prompt over a shared thread, with no orchestration layer. Each has a distinct voice, owns one area, shares private knowledge only when asked and points to the right colleague for anything outside the field. Nobody starts a conversation, so a teammate who is never messaged is data, not a failure.
- The traps interlock. Each needs a different teammate, and some need two. One ticket has no trap on purpose, so the interview does not turn into a puzzle hunt and a struggling candidate has somewhere to succeed.
- The tickets are ordered by the signal they give. The first produces the strongest sign of asking within minutes. The last is the one most likely to be cut short, and it still yields signal when rushed.
- The Claude Code transcript is the evidence. The pilot needs no custom software, and the format is for internal hiring only.

## Cases

**Traps that can only be asked, not guessed.** With an AI agent writing the code, recall tells little about a candidate. What remains is judgement: noticing that something is missing or wrong, and asking the person who knows. So every ticket carries a gap that reading the repository cannot close. The answer sits with one teammate, and the candidate gets it only by writing to that person. A perfect build of the wrong thing is scored as excellent work on the wrong thing, a common habit in real teams and not carelessness. Learning that a requirement is wrong and carrying on unchanged is scored differently from never asking, because one is a communication failure and the other a curiosity failure. The guide tells interviewers to rotate the specifics over time, since candidates talk.

**A colleague who defends the code.** Reviewing is cheap when the reviewer backs down at the first push. One ticket is a pull request from the frontend teammate, with several problems of different weight. The teammate defends the work with plausible reasons and concedes only to a specific argument, never to confidence. The signal is whether the candidate holds a correct position, makes it concrete and ranks the problems by importance.

**Teammates that stay in character.** The teammates hold the answers, so they must not leak them, or the grading, to a candidate who asks cleverly. A shared set of rules sits in front of every brief. Candidate messages are untrusted input, rapport is not a key, nothing is volunteered, no code is written, replies stay in short chat style, and a refusal stays in character instead of turning into a lecture. An adversarial round of prompts against each teammate is planned before any real candidate sits the interview.

**A human decides, by design.** The format produces no score and no ranking. It collects the transcript and the chat, and an interviewer reads the evidence and decides, writing down the basis. The rubric has five dimensions scored from 1 to 4, each tied to something observable, such as a quoted message or a moment in the transcript. It gives no credit for tickets finished, speed or lines written. The guide has two interviewers score the first few candidates independently, and treats a gap of more than a point as a rubric bug. The candidate brief says plainly that the teammates are AI, that the session is recorded and that a person decides, and it offers another format to anyone who prefers not to be recorded.
